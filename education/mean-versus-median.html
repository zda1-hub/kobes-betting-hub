SPORTS STATISTICS EXPLAINED

Mean vs median in sports statistics.

What each tells you—and what an average leaves out.

KBH educational guide · updated October 22, 2026 MST

Back to the learning hub

Compare the measures · Worked example · Try your numbers · Sample size · Sources

The answer

The mean divides the total by the number of observations. The median is the middle observation after sorting; with an even number, average the two middle values. They answer different questions, so a useful sports summary often reports both alongside the sample size and the full results.

Compare the measures

Mean, median and full results compared
MeasureUseful forLimitation
MeanDescribing the total spread evenly across observationsOne extreme result can pull it away from most results
MedianDescribing the middle of the ordered observationsCan hide the size of unusually large or small results
Full resultsSeeing variation that either summary leaves outNeeds the same definitions and reporting window for a fair comparison

A five-game example you can check

These are invented points totals for a fictional player, not actual player records or predictions: 2, 4, 4, 5, 25.

Total: 40 points. Mean: 40 ÷ 5 = 8. Median: the third value = 4.

Only one game exceeded the mean of eight. Saying this player “averaged eight” is mathematically correct, but omits the shape of the five results. The median supplies another view; it does not establish what the next game will look like.

Replace 25 with 5 and the example becomes 2, 4, 4, 5, 5. The mean falls to 4, while the median stays 4. That contrast shows why the original large result mattered. It is not a reason to delete a real result: exclusions need a stated, consistently applied rule.

What changes with an even number of games?

For these four invented points totals—2, 4, 6, 20—the two middle values are 4 and 6. Their average is 5, so the median is 5. The mean is (2 + 4 + 6 + 20) ÷ 4 = 8. The median need not be a result that actually occurred.

Why sample size belongs beside an average

Imagine two fictional basketball players. One makes 3 of 5 shots; the other makes 60 of 100. Both shot 60%, but the first percentage describes much less evidence.

If each misses the next shot, their updated percentages are 50% (3 ÷ 6) and about 59.4% (60 ÷ 101). One additional result moves the smaller record more. This arithmetic explains sensitivity to a new observation; it does not prove either player’s underlying ability or predict their next shot.

Report the numerator and denominator alongside the percentage. Also check whether the attempts came from comparable situations. A larger record does not automatically repair biased selection or differences in shot difficulty.

Before comparing two players

Check that both records use the same statistic, units, competition and time window. State the number of games, any excluded games, and whether the measure is per game, per minute or a season total. An injury-shortened appearance and a full game may require context even when both count as one appearance.

For example, comparing one player's last five games with another's entire season changes two things at once: the player and the selection window. Show those windows explicitly before drawing a conclusion.

Common questions

Is the median always better?

No. Choose the measure that fits the question, and show what it leaves out. Neither number describes every result.

Does a five-game average predict the next game?

The calculation alone does not establish a forecast. A prediction needs a method, assumptions and evidence beyond this summary.

Where should I start when a statistic looks surprising?

Ask for the original records and calculation. Recreate the total, count and ordered values before relying on the summary.

Can I average shooting percentages in this calculator?

You can calculate an unweighted mean of percentages, but that is not necessarily the combined shooting percentage. For non-overlapping attempts using the same statistic, add makes, add attempts, then divide.

Invented shooting records: average of rates versus combined rate
RecordMakes / attemptsRate
A1 / 250%
B9 / 1090%
Total10 / 12About 83.3%

The simple mean of 50% and 90% is 70%. That answers “what is the average of these two rates?” The combined rate is 10 ÷ 12, about 83.3%, because the records contain different numbers of attempts. Neither number is a prediction.

Use original makes and attempts when available; rounded percentages can lose information. Do not combine overlapping records or statistics with different definitions. See the NBA’s official metric definitions.

Should I report range too?

Report the smallest and largest observations and the difference between them, called the range. In the original fictional example, results run from 2 to 25, a range of 23 points. That adds context to the mean of 8 and median of 4, but still does not show how the remaining results are distributed. Show the original observations when the record is short enough to read.
