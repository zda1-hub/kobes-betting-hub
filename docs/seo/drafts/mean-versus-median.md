# Mean vs median in sports statistics: what each tells you

Editorial draft · October 21, 2026 MST · Not published

Proposed path: /education/mean-versus-median

Proposed description: Compare mean and median with a five-game example, see how one large result changes an average, and learn what to check before trusting a sports statistic.

## The answer

The mean divides the total by the number of observations. The median is the middle observation after sorting; with an even number, average the two middle values. They answer different questions, so a useful sports summary often reports both alongside the sample size and the full results.

## Compare the measures

| Measure | Useful for | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mean | Describing the total spread evenly across observations | One extreme result can pull it away from most results |
| Median | Describing the middle of the ordered observations | Can hide the size of unusually large or small results |
| Full results | Seeing variation that either summary leaves out | Needs the same definitions and reporting window for a fair comparison |

## A five-game example you can check

These are invented points totals for a fictional player, not actual player records or predictions: **2, 4, 4, 5, 25**.

Total: 40 points. Mean: 40 ÷ 5 = **8**. Median: the third value = **4**.

Only one game exceeded the mean of eight. Saying this player “averaged eight” is mathematically correct, but omits the shape of the five results. The median supplies another view; it does not establish what the next game will look like.

Replace 25 with 5 and the example becomes 2, 4, 4, 5, 5. The mean falls to **4**, while the median stays **4**. That contrast shows why the original large result mattered. It is not a reason to delete a real result: exclusions need a stated, consistently applied rule.

## Before comparing two players

Check that both records use the same statistic, units, competition and time window. State the number of games, any excluded games, and whether the measure is per game, per minute or a season total. An injury-shortened appearance and a full game may require context even when both count as one appearance.

For example, comparing one player's last five games with another's entire season changes two things at once: the player and the selection window. Show those windows explicitly before drawing a conclusion.

## Common questions

### Is the median always better?

No. Choose the measure that fits the question, and show what it leaves out. Neither number describes every result.

### Does a five-game average predict the next game?

The calculation alone does not establish a forecast. A prediction needs a method, assumptions and evidence beyond this summary.

### Where should I start when a statistic looks surprising?

Ask for the original records and calculation. Recreate the total, count and ordered values before relying on the summary.
